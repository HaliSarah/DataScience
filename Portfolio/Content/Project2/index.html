Forecasting Next-Day Hourly Electricity Demand in ERCOT

Grid operators have to schedule power before anyone flips a switch. This project asks how close public historical data — past demand, calendar patterns and observed temperatures — can get to the forecast ERCOT itself publishes for the next day.

  • 41,616 hourly records
  • 3.54% MAPE, best team model
  • EIA-930 data, 2022–2026
Watch the presentation Code & notebooks

Business Problem

Schedule first, consume later

Accurate short-term demand forecasts matter because generation schedules, energy purchases and reserve decisions are set before the electricity is used. Over-forecast and the grid commits power it doesn't need; under-forecast and it leans on short-notice market adjustments. The February 2021 Texas crisis showed how costly a stressed grid can be, though outages can't be blamed on forecasts alone.

ERCOT is a useful test case because EIA reports both realized hourly demand and ERCOT's own day-ahead forecast, so every model can be scored against an independent operational benchmark, not just a simple baseline.

Research question: Can historical demand, calendar patterns and selected weather variables forecast ERCOT's next-day hourly demand with accuracy approaching the operator's published forecast?

Data source
U.S. Energy Information Administration, Form EIA-930 (hourly ERCOT grid data); Open-Meteo weather archive
Study period
January 2022 – September 2026
Target
Hourly demand in megawatthours (MWh)
Test window
October 2025 – September 2026 (8,760 hours)
Benchmark
ERCOT's published day-ahead forecast

Hypothesis

What we expected going in

Models that capture recurring time patterns — hour of day, weekday, season, and demand at the same hour yesterday and last week — should clearly beat a baseline that simply repeats the same hour from the previous week. Weather was added as a second test: does temperature give the models anything extra?

My part: data wrangling

I pulled the hourly series from the EIA API, checked it for duplicates, missing hours and impossible values, and kept a cleaning log. The demand series arrived complete, so no value was changed or filled. I then built the calendar features and the 24- and 168-hour demand lags, and merged on temperatures from Houston, Dallas–Fort Worth, San Antonio and Austin, population-weighted into one Texas temperature with heating and cooling indicators.

Two decisions worth noting

UTC timestamps keep every hour unique through daylight-saving changes, while calendar features use Central time because demand follows the local clock. No lag shorter than 24 hours is used, since a day-ahead forecast can't know the most recent day. A spike rule that flagged two real overnight lows was loosened rather than deleting them.

Models: a seasonal-naive baseline (same hour last week), rolling 24-hour SARIMA and weather-enhanced SARIMAX, Prophet, XGBoost with and without weather, and a 50/50 hybrid of rolling SARIMA and weather XGBoost. All were tested on a chronological holdout, and scored on 8,640 hours shared with ERCOT (120 hours of ERCOT's forecast were unavailable and not filled in).

Results

Beat the baseline, not the operator

The hypothesis held: history and calendar patterns clearly outperform repeating last week. The 50/50 hybrid of rolling SARIMA and weather-enhanced XGBoost was the strongest team model overall, at roughly 2,006 MWh average error (3.54% MAPE) against ERCOT's 1,135 MWh (2.00%).

Weather didn't help everywhere. It improved XGBoost, but the weather-enhanced SARIMAX did slightly worse than plain rolling SARIMA — the value of weather depends on the model.

The Bigger Finding

Average accuracy hid the weakness that matters

The model that looked best across the year was not the one to trust during the hours operators worry about most. In the top 1% of demand hours, rolling SARIMA and weather SARIMAX came within roughly 100 MWh of ERCOT, while the hybrid’s error was more than two and a half times larger.

These peak hours were identified after the fact, so the result diagnoses a historical weakness; it doesn't predict which future hours will be extreme.

Recommendations & Ethical Considerations

Who this affects — and where it could go wrong

Stakeholder impact

Grid operators and utilities could use an independent check on demand forecasts. Households and businesses are affected indirectly: under-forecasting peak demand can contribute to shortages and price spikes, which fall hardest on people relying on medical equipment or air conditioning during heat waves.

Data assumptions & bias

The models assume past patterns continue, which rapid load growth breaks. Observed temperatures also assume perfect weather knowledge, overstating what a real forecast could achieve. The data is public, aggregated to the whole grid, and identifies no customers.

Misuse risk

A single accuracy number could be read as proof a model is ready for operations, when it did worst in the hours that matter most. Results shouldn't be applied to other grids without retraining, or presented as real-time performance.

Recommendations

Choose models on overall accuracy and demand conditions. Report peak-hour error and bias next to the average. Future versions should use advance weather forecasts and a fixed issue time, and any model should support operator judgment, not replace it.

Conclusion

Back to the real question

History and the calendar get surprisingly far: a hybrid built only from public data cut the baseline's error by about half. But ERCOT's forecast stayed well ahead, and the most useful result wasn't the leaderboard — it was that a strong yearly average can conceal peak-hour weakness.

A good average isn't the same as a reliable forecast — especially when demand is highest.

Limits and next steps: models were scored on the same hours but not under identical conditions (issue times and inputs differ, and observed weather isn't an advance forecast). Next: standardize issue times, use only advance-available inputs, and test model weights that adapt to conditions known at forecast time.
