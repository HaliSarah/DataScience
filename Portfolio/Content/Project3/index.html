Community Characteristics and NYC Recycling Performance

New York City’s 59 community districts send very different shares of their waste to dry recycling. This project asks what community characteristics line up with those differences, whether they can flag the districts most in need of attention, and how well next year’s share can be forecast.

  • 59 community districts
  • 43% less error with income alone
  • DSNY tonnage, 2005–2026
See the presentation Code & notebooks

Business Problem

Citywide averages hide neighborhood differences

The Department of Sanitation (DSNY) collects refuse and recyclables in every community district, but the share that is dry recycling (paper plus metal, glass and plastic) swings from 5.1% in Bronx CD 2 to 28.4% in Brooklyn CD 6. A citywide figure of 16.9% hides all of that.

A lower share doesn’t automatically mean residents recycle less. Refuse volume, housing, composting participation and collection practices all move the percentage. DSNY needs evidence that helps decide where to look and where to do outreach, without unfairly labeling communities.

Four questions: (1) How does share vary across districts and boroughs? (2) Which community characteristics are associated with a lower share? (3) Can those characteristics, or history, flag the highest-risk districts? (4) How accurately can monthly share be forecast a year ahead?

My part: modeling

I engineered the district features and built the models for questions 2 to 4. With only 59 districts, I scored every model with repeated cross-validation (tuning inside each fold so no district graded itself), and compared each against a simple baseline. For forecasting I used a chronological split, training on 2005–2023, tuning on 2024 and testing on 2025 onward, and added a Temporal Fusion Transformer after instructor feedback.

Decisions worth noting

Income source: the Planning ACS income was chosen on data quality before any model scores were seen, and the other source was kept as a sensitivity check. No leakage: refuse, paper and MGP tonnage make up the target, so none became a feature. Date-aware lags: a teammate caught that shifting by row position pairs the wrong months when records are missing, so lags are matched on calendar date.

Models: an average-share baseline, income-only and all-feature linear regression, ridge and random forest for question 2; last year’s lowest districts, logistic regression and random forest for question 3; and a seasonal-naive baseline against ridge, gradient boosting (with and without district features), a Temporal Fusion Transformer and an ensemble for question 4.

Results · Community characteristics

Income is the signal

In the full regression (R² = 0.68), a district one standard deviation higher in income had about 4.8 points more recycling share (95% interval 3.7 to 6.0). The other four features were uncertain once income was accounted for, and the result held with a second income source, without a possibly top-coded Manhattan district, and in the random forest’s importance ranking.

This is an observational pattern across districts. It doesn’t show that income causes the difference, or say anything about individual households.

Areas for further investigation

Beyond income, something else is going on

A large gap means something beyond income may be holding a district’s share down, such as building stock, collection setup or awareness. That makes these districts the best places to ask questions first.

They are leads, not verdicts. A gap is not proof of poor resident behavior, and it doesn’t identify a cause.

Results · High-risk districts

The best predictor was the past

“High risk” meant the bottom quarter of 2025 share, 15 districts. Because DSNY would act on a short list, each method was scored on how many of the true 15 it placed in its own top 15.

The practical reading: flag districts using last year’s ranking, then use community characteristics to help explain why. Where the two lists disagree is where a closer look is most worthwhile.

Results · Forecasting

Short-term forecasts held up; long-term ones were modest

Adding district characteristics helped gradient boosting a little, which fits the earlier finding that those characteristics explain which districts are high or low far better than how they will change.

With only 59 short series, the more flexible network had too much room to chase noise. Reporting it honestly, as tested but not chosen, is part of the result.

The Bigger Finding

A citywide shift no district trait could predict

From April 2025, actual collection shares ran about one percentage point above every forecast. Compared with the same months of 2024, the data show a change in what was being collected.

+158%compost collection, Apr–Oct 2025 vs. 2024
−3.7%refuse collection, same months
+3–4%paper and metal, glass and plastic, same months

The drop in refuse alone accounts for about half of the one-point rise. A change in collection practices, such as expanded composting, may help explain the gap, but this analysis can’t confirm it. A higher collection share also doesn’t necessarily mean more material was recycled or recovered.

Recommendations & Ethical Considerations

Who this affects — and where it could go wrong

Stakeholder impact

DSNY could use these results to decide where to investigate and do outreach. Residents are affected indirectly: a district labeled “low recycling” can attract blame it hasn’t earned, especially when income, housing and collection setup are driving the number.

Data assumptions & bias

Everything is aggregated to districts, so nothing here describes individual behavior. Community indicators come from different geographic proxies and reporting periods: DSNY uses 59 districts, some sources use 55 areas, and 46 of 59 income areas only approximate district boundaries. One Manhattan income value looks top-coded.

Misuse risk

Collection share is an operational indicator, not a direct measure of how much is recycled. It shouldn’t be used to rank neighborhoods or assign blame, and the forecasts shouldn’t be trusted through program changes the models have never seen.

Recommendations

Investigate districts below their income-based expectation. Use last year’s ranking to flag persistently low districts, and pair forecasts with awareness of collection changes. Support targeted outreach and a review of local conditions, and communicate the uncertainty alongside the numbers.

Conclusion

Back to the real question

Income explains most of the difference between districts, history is the best way to flag the lowest ones, and a year-ahead forecast is only modestly better than repeating last year. The most useful output isn’t a ranking, it’s a short list of places to ask better questions.

Use the results to guide questions — not to assign blame.

Limits and next steps: the analysis is observational and district-level, and its income and housing indicators are approximations of district boundaries. Next steps are to study the 2025 collection change directly as program data becomes available, and to test whether the below-income districts share building or collection conditions that outreach could address.
